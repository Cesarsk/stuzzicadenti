Types of Stuzzicadenti

Every culture, every era — a different pick for a different purpose.

🧅

Classic Round

USA / Global

The most common toothpick worldwide. First mass-produced in 1869 by Charles Forster in Strong, Maine using birch wood.

📌

Flat Paddle

Europe

Wider and sturdier than round picks. Perfect for appetizers and small bites. Often found in Swiss restaurants.

🍹

Cocktail Pick

Global / Bar Culture

Decorative picks with ornate ends — umbrellas, swords, or fruit shapes. Essential for garnishing martinis and appetizers.

🎌

Tsumayoji

Japan

Japanese-style pointed picks, often with a decorative groove at one end that doubles as a rest. Considered an art form in Japanese dining.

🌿

Flavored Pick

USA / Modern

Infused with cinnamon, tea tree, or mint oils. Popular as a smoking cessation aid and breath freshener since the 1990s.

💎

Luxury Metal

Ancient Rome / Modern

Romans used gold and silver toothpicks as status symbols. Today, titanium and stainless steel picks are eco-friendly alternatives.

A Brief History

The toothpick is one of humanity's oldest tools — older than our species itself.

1.8 Million Years Ago

Prehistoric Origins

Grooves found in the teeth of Homo habilis skulls suggest the use of thin sticks or bone splinters for dental cleaning — making the toothpick potentially the oldest human tool.

Ancient Greece & Rome

Status Symbols

Wealthy Romans carried ornate toothpicks made of gold, silver, or bronze. Pliny the Elder recommended porcupine quills. In Greece, they were part of every gentleman's grooming kit.

17th Century

European Elegance

Toothpicks became fashionable accessories in European courts. Elaborate toothpick cases were crafted from gold, enamel, and precious stones. Queen Elizabeth I received a gold toothpick as a gift.

1869, Maine, USA

Mass Production

Charles Forster founded the first industrial toothpick factory in Strong, Maine. He hired Harvard students to eat at restaurants and loudly demand toothpicks to create demand. By 1887, his factory produced millions daily.

Japan, Traditional

The Art of Tsumayoji

Japanese tsumayoji are pointed at one end with a decorative groove at the other. The groove serves as a chopstick rest. Kuromoji (spicebush) wood is preferred for its subtle fragrance and antibacterial properties.

Today

Modern Revival

From flavored picks to eco-friendly bamboo, the toothpick continues to evolve. The global market exceeds $1 billion. Cocktail culture has made decorative picks an art form again.
